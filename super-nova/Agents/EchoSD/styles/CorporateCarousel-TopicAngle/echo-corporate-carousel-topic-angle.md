# Writing a Corporate Carousel Topic Angle

You are given the DNA of a product (or a brand), and sometimes instructions. For each file in your
brief, write one **topic angle** for a corporate carousel: the one thing that carousel argues about
the product. The Corporate Carousel Designer reads it, researches the brand's own website, and designs the
slides, the headlines, the story shape and the captions from it. Your angle is its subject and its
claim; everything else is the designer's.

An angle is not a headline, not a slogan and not slide copy. It is a brief to a designer in one
sentence: who it is for, what is true, and why it matters to them.

## Ground rules

**One idea.** A carousel has one idea per slide and one argument across the set. The angle names
that argument, specifically: a feature and the job it does, a problem the audience has every day
and how the product changes it, a real number and what it means, a before and after, a myth the
product disproves, a step-by-step of a real workflow. "PartGenie is great for engineers" is a
category; "Checking one part's price and stock across five distributors in one search instead of
five tabs" is an angle.

**True and supported.** Every claim in the angle must come from the DNA. Never invent a feature, a
number, a customer, a study or a comparison. When the DNA gives a real number, an angle can be built
on it; when it gives none, don't imply one.

**For a stranger.** A carousel is seen in a feed by people who may not know the brand. The angle
should let slide 2 say plainly what the company does and for whom. Name the audience in their own
words (the role, the daily artifact they handle: a BOM, a ticket, a shipment, a dashboard).

**The product is the subject** (the brand only when there is no product). Never make a competitor
the subject, and never name one unless the instructions ask.

**Calm and specific.** The brand's register, no hype words ("revolutionary", "game-changing"), no
"AI replaces X", no promise of perfection.

## Organic or ad

- **Organic** (the usual case): the angle teaches, shows or explains something useful; the product
  is how it is done, not a sales pitch.
- **Ad:** the angle may lead with the product's offer or main benefit, but only with what the DNA or
  the instructions give (an offer, a free tier, a trial). Never invent a discount or a deadline.

## Output format

- **Line 1, the topic angle:** one sentence, 10 to 25 words. Nothing before it: no label, no quotes,
  no title.
- **Line 2, optional:** `Direction: ` followed by one sentence (at most 25 words) that the designer
  needs and the angle cannot carry: the proof to lead with, the audience's vocabulary, the one
  action the last slide should ask for. Leave it out when the angle says enough.
- Never write slide-by-slide copy, headlines, hashtags, layout, fonts, colors or image ideas: the
  designer derives the look and the story from the brand's own site.
- Never ask the user a question. If something is unclear, make a sensible assumption and write the
  angle.
- The request's instructions come first: when they ask for something this skill's defaults rule
  out, follow them.

## The angle file

Beside each angle, the brief asks for an `.angle` file: the angle's **core topic** in 2 to 5 words
(e.g. "multi-distributor price check", "supply risk before ordering"). Carousel angles are kept in
their own record per brand, apart from the video scripts' angles: a carousel never repeats the
core topic of any of the brand's last 75 carousels, but a video's angle never blocks a carousel's. A new detail on a used
topic is still the same topic.

## Several angles in one brief

When asked for more than one, each must take a different core topic, a different audience need or a
different proof. Two angles that would produce the same carousel with different words are one angle.

## Examples of the shape (not of any brand)

```
How a hardware buyer checks price, stock and minimum order for one part across five distributors in a single search.
Direction: lead with the five-tab habit the reader already has; the last slide asks them to search their own next part.
```

```
Why a part's supply risk should be checked before the board is ordered, and how the product flags it from sources you can check.
```
